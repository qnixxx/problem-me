01 / INCIDENT REPORT

A very thirsty week

The Bureau's little garden experienced fifty recorded watering disruptions in a fictional seven-day period. A hose connector leaked, somebody left a tap open and the ducks were accused of splashing.

Lilac refuses to fix everything at once. First she asks what measure is consistent across the log. Here it is the number of incidents—not litres of water or repair cost.

MEASURE: incident count. SCOPE: fictional seven-day log; 50 incidents.

02 / RANK THE RECORDS

The largest bars come first

The team sorts five categories: loose connectors 21, open taps 13, spilled cans 8, sun/heat alerts 5, duck splashes 3. The totals add to fifty, with each share calculated using the same denominator.

The largest category accounts for 42%. The first two contribute 34 of fifty, or 68%; the first three contribute 42 of fifty, or 84%. See the ranked breakdown below.

03 / READ THE PATTERN

Pareto isn't a magical 80/20 rule

The data don't follow an exact 80/20 split, and that's fine. The method looks for where the burden concentrates. Connector problems top the frequency ranking. Inspecting connectors makes sense if reducing the number of disruptions is the goal.

One tap left open all night could still waste more water than many small drips. If the aim changes to water volume, the team needs volume data and a new ranking.

CAUTION: choose the measure that matches the decision.

04 / FIRST IMPROVEMENT

Prioritize, then investigate

The Bureau checks connectors, replaces worn seals where confirmed and tracks new incidents. It also introduces a shut-off checklist for the second-ranked open-tap category.

Pareto helps decide where to start, but it doesn't reveal why connectors fail. Fishbone or 5 Whys could examine the winning category next.

TAKEAWAY: rank impact first; establish cause separately.
