THE PICNIC WITH TOO MANY GUESTS_

The ants arrived on time. The explanation didn't.

01 / INCIDENT REPORT

An uninvited procession

Ten minutes into Capybara's picnic, a line of ants reached the blanket. Mango blamed the jam. Lilac suspected the bin. Pebble offered to build a fence with a security gate. Nobody had inspected the ground.

The observable problem is ants reaching the blanket after food was unpacked. The cause remains uncertain.

EFFECT: ants near food ten minutes after setup.

02 / FISHBONE MAP

Draw possibilities before verdicts

Food: spilled sweet juice, jam crumbs, uncovered fruit. Location: an existing ant trail or a nearby bin. Conditions: warm weather and a windy blanket corner. Routine: food left exposed while everyone unpacked.

These branches organize hypotheses. A Fishbone diagram is a list of places to look—not evidence that every factor contributed.

HYPOTHESES: food, location, conditions and setup routine.

03 / EVIDENCE CHECK

See what's different

In this invented observation, the crew notices a trail between the bin and the blanket, a small juice spill at the edge, and no visible ants on a clean mat several metres away. Covered food remains untouched during the check.

Those details make location and accessible sweetness plausible contributors. They do not establish one exclusive cause. To test further, clean the spill, move away from the known trail and observe again without harming the insects.

SEEN: trail and spill. NOT SEEN: same trail at comparison mat.

04 / BETTER NEXT PICNIC

Narrow the map using evidence

The crew relocates the blanket, closes drink lids and covers leftovers. It checks whether those changes reduce visitors next time. If not, Pebble can revisit the map rather than invent a more elaborate border fence.

Fishbone helps when several explanations compete. Evidence—not the confidence of the loudest capybara—does the narrowing.

TAKEAWAY: a diagram of suspects is not a verdict.
