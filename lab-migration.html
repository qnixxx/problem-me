STAGE 01 / NON-NEGOTIABLE CONSTRAINTS

The lowest price may not be eligible.

The decision owner sets three explicit MUST criteria: (1) EU-only data residency, (2) recovery time objective demonstrated at or below 60 minutes, and (3) complete data export demonstrated within 48 hours. These are illustrative exercise constraints, not legal claims about all businesses.

DECISION / WHICH OPTIONS ARE ELIGIBLE?

A — Exclude C, then compare A and B

Correct screening. C fails a stated non-negotiable condition. Its attractive price or recovery result cannot compensate for ineligibility. A and B meet the verified MUSTs and can advance to preference scoring.

B — Allow C because the low cost is overwhelmingly attractive

Invalid trade-off. A MUST is an eligibility condition, not a weighted preference. Reconsidering a legitimate requirement is a separate governance decision, not something a high score can silently override.

STAGE 02 / MAKE PREFERENCES EXPLICIT

Scoring helps, but cannot decide for you.

The stakeholders score eligible options from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent). Higher is better. These values reflect fictional judgments and must be challenged for bias and sensitivity.

Totals: A = 5×5 + 3×3 + 2×2 = 38. B = 5×2 + 3×4 + 2×5 = 32.

EVIDENCE C / Sensitivity check

Suppose customization turns out to matter more than stakeholders first assumed, increasing its weight from 2 to 5. Recalculate:

Managed A: 5×5 + 3×3 + 5×2 = 44.
Self-hosted B: 5×2 + 3×4 + 5×5 = 47.

The ranking reverses. A six-point preference advantage under the original weights is not a robust proof of the “best” option.

DECISION / HOW DO YOU INTERPRET 38 VERSUS 32?

A — Prefer A provisionally, then test sensitivity and risks

Good judgment. A leads under the stated preferences, but B can lead when priorities shift. Confirm stakeholder values, assess actual downside risk and make an explicit decision record before committing.

Continue to risk planning →
B — Announce that 38 mathematically proves A is objectively best

False precision. Multiplying subjective weights by subjective ratings provides a transparent comparison, not an objective fact. A sensitivity analysis shows the conclusion can reverse.

STAGE 03 / ANTICIPATE THE DOWNSIDE

A promising option still needs protective actions.

The exercise decision owner leans toward A because of lower operating effort, but worries about export lock-in and a difficult rollback if service recovery fails in real operations. The earlier tests meet stated MUSTs, but system change still creates new risk.

DECISION / WHAT MAKES THE PREFERRED OPTION SAFER?

A — Pilot a representative migration, export, recovery and rollback rehearsal

Risk-aware choice. Establish measurable acceptance criteria before contracting. Run a complete pilot on representative data, verify recovery and exit routes, and decide on triggers that stop or reverse the migration. Reassess whether the preference advantage remains credible.

Continue to the debrief →
B — Sign immediately; the requirements and scores are enough

Under-controlled. Passing a test does not mean all future changes will be harmless. Review contractual exit rights, operational readiness, new dependencies and a tested rollback path before high-impact commitment.

> lab-03 / decision-review

Debrief: eligibility before preference.

  • MUSTs: EU residency, documented recovery objective and tested full export. C fails the residency requirement and is excluded.
  • WANTs: A scores 38, B scores 32 under the original preferences; this is not a universally correct or objective ranking.
  • Sensitivity: customization weight 5 reverses the ranking (A = 44, B = 47), revealing where stakeholder values matter.
  • Downside: exit difficulty, recovery uncertainty, contractual dependencies and hidden operating costs.
  • Protection and verification: representative migration rehearsal, complete export and recovery tests, rollback criteria, post-migration service objectives and a scheduled review.
TRANSFER THE METHOD_

Kepner–Tregoe-style decision analysis makes assumptions visible. It does not erase subjective judgments or the obligation to evaluate the risks of a preferred alternative.
